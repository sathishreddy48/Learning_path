Levels & the Decision

The ladder every large company runs, what the mid-to-senior jump actually measures, how to calibrate yourself honestly, and what happens after the loop — because the decision is made from written evidence by people who may never have met you.

On this page

  1. What levels mean
  2. Targeting a level, and down-level risk
  3. How the decision is actually made

What levels mean

Every large company runs a level ladder, and the numbers differ but the rungs map onto each other closely. The level you interview at determines the bar you are held to, the loop you get, the offer band, and — more than anything else — what the interviewers are listening for.

The common ladder, with the usual naming at a few companies. Treat the year ranges as very rough; level is about demonstrated scope, not time served, and companies differ in how they calibrate.

Senior Staff+Org- or company-wide technical strategy.12+ yrsSenior Principal · L7+ · E7+

The jump that matters, and the one most candidates misjudge, is mid to senior. It is not "writes better code". It is a change in the unit of work: a mid-level engineer is given a problem and solves it; a senior engineer is given an ambiguous situation, decides what the problem actually is, and brings other people with them. Everything in the senior loop is calibrated to that distinction.

SignalMid-level answerSenior answer
A vague requirementAsks what to build, then builds itWorks out what is actually needed, and says what not to build
A design trade-offPicks the technically better optionPicks against the constraints — team, timeline, operability — and says why
Something went wrongFixed itFixed it, then changed something so the class of problem cannot recur
DisagreementEscalated, or went along with itFramed the disagreement in terms of the outcome and got to a decision
Impact described as"I built X""We needed Y; X was the cheapest way to get it; here is what it moved"
Interview angle

The practical consequence is that at senior level the deep-dive and behavioural rounds are where the level is set, and the coding round is mostly a filter — you need to not fail it, but passing it brilliantly will not move you up a level. Candidates coming from a strong algorithms background frequently over-invest in the part that cannot help them. If you are targeting senior, the honest question to ask yourself is whether you can tell three stories about ambiguity you resolved rather than problems you solved, because that is the thing being assessed.

Practice
  • Write down the level you are targeting, and then three pieces of evidence from your own work for the scope expectation in the table.
  • If you cannot produce them, that is useful information: either target a rung lower, or go and get the evidence before you interview.

Targeting a level, and down-level risk

Interviewing at the wrong level is one of the most expensive mistakes available, in both directions, and it is largely under your control.

  • Too high and you are measured against a bar you cannot clear. Sometimes this converts into an offer one level down; often it is a rejection, with a cooldown before you can try again.
  • Too low and you will probably get the offer, at a band you may spend years climbing out of. Internal promotion is usually slower than changing level by changing jobs.

Down-levelling is the outcome where you interview for senior, do not clear that bar, and are offered the rung below. Whether this is even possible depends on the company and on whether the req is open at both levels — which is exactly why asking your recruiter what level the req is open at matters so much.

How to calibrate honestly

Read the company's published engineering levels if they have them, and read job postings at the level above and below the one you want. Then ask the mid-versus-senior question in the table above about your own last two years, and answer it with evidence rather than with how you feel. The most reliable external check is to ask someone currently at that level at a comparable company to read your résumé and say which rung it reads as. People are usually willing, and the answer is often half a level away from the candidate's own estimate in one direction or the other.

Cooldowns

Most large companies impose a waiting period before you can interview again after a rejection — commonly in the range of six to twelve months, and it varies by company, by outcome and over time. This is the main argument for not interviewing at your top-choice company first. Do two or three loops at companies you like less to get the format out of your system, and go to the one you actually want fourth, when your interviewing is sharp. The difference between a first loop and a fourth loop is large and it is almost entirely about nerves and pacing rather than knowledge.

Interview angle

If you are genuinely between levels, it is usually worth interviewing at the higher one where the req allows it, as long as you can live with a rejection and the cooldown. The asymmetry is that being down-levelled to an offer is a normal, recoverable outcome, whereas accepting a level below what you could have had is a slow, compounding cost that is hard to correct from inside. But make the choice deliberately and with the recruiter's input rather than by default — and if you are told the req is open at only one level, that settles it.

Practice
  • Ask someone at your target level, at a comparable company, to read your résumé and tell you which rung it reads as.
  • Order your target companies so your top choice is not your first loop.

How the decision is actually made

You do not get hired by the people who interviewed you, in most large companies. Understanding what happens after the loop changes how you behave during it.

  1. Each interviewer writes up independently, usually before seeing anyone else's feedback, against a rubric with per-axis scores and a recommendation. Independence is deliberate — it stops one strong opinion anchoring everyone.
  2. A debrief or committee reviews the packet. At some companies this is the interviewers in a room; at others it is a separate committee of people who never met you, reading only what was written. Amazon's Bar Raiser is a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team with the standing to block a hire — the role exists specifically to stop teams lowering the bar because they are desperate.
  3. The outcome is a hire/no-hire plus a level, and often those are decided separately.
What this means for you, concretely

Three things follow.

  • The written record is what is judged, not your charm. If your reasoning was not said out loud, it was not written down, and it does not exist. This is the strongest possible argument for narrating your thinking in every round.
  • Each round is scored independently. A bad first round does not doom you, because the next interviewer very often has not seen it. Candidates who mentally write off the day after one weak round convert a recoverable loop into a lost one — this is common and it is entirely self-inflicted.
  • One strong "no hire" can block an otherwise good packet, especially from a bar-raiser-type role. Consistency across rounds matters more than one spectacular performance.
Typical recommendationRoughly means
Strong hireClearly above the bar for the level; would actively seek them out
HireAbove the bar
No hireNot demonstrated at this level — sometimes paired with "would hire one level down"
Strong no hireA clear problem, often a red flag rather than a skills gap

The exact labels differ between companies; the structure — a graded recommendation plus written evidence — is near-universal.

Interview angle

The behaviour this should change: make the interviewer's write-up easy. They will be at a keyboard shortly after you leave, trying to reconstruct what you did. Everything you said clearly, every trade-off you named, every edge case you caught yourself, is a line they can write. A candidate who solved the problem in silence leaves them with "got the right answer, could not assess the reasoning" — which is a much weaker packet than it feels like from the inside. At the end of a round, a brief summary of what you built and what you would do with more time is a genuine gift to the person writing you up, and it costs thirty seconds.

Practice
  • After your next mock, write the feedback form yourself as if you were the interviewer. Notice how little you could actually say.
  • Practise closing each round with a 30-second summary: what you built, the complexity or trade-offs, and one thing you would change.
