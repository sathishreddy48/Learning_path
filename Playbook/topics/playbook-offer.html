Offer & Negotiation

How big-tech compensation is put together

An offer is not a salary, and reading it as one is how people accept worse packages than they were offered. Four components, which behave completely differently.

ComponentWhat it isBehaviour over timeHow negotiable
Base salaryCash, paid per pay periodRises slowly; usually banded tightly by levelLeast — often the most constrained piece
Equity (RSUs)Stock granted at the offer, vesting over ~4 yearsValue moves with the share priceUsually the most negotiable
Signing bonusOne-off cash, often paid across year 1 (sometimes year 2)Does not recurQuite negotiable — it is the easiest lever to pull
Annual bonusA target percentage of base, performance-adjustedVaries; some companies fold it into base insteadRarely negotiable — it is formulaic

Two structural features matter more than most people realise:

  • Vesting schedules are not all the same. A four-year grant may vest evenly (25/25/25/25), or front-load, or back-load. A back-loaded schedule means a headline four-year number that you only reach if you stay the full term — which makes year-one and year-two totals far more meaningful for comparison than the headline.
  • The cliff. Most grants vest nothing until a first milestone, commonly one year. Leaving before it usually means the equity is forfeited entirely.
  • Refreshers. Your initial grant decays as it vests. Companies issue additional grants over time to compensate, but policies vary widely and refreshers are rarely guaranteed in writing. A large initial grant with no refresher culture can mean a noticeable pay cut in year five.
Compare year-one and steady-state totals, not headline numbers

A "$400k offer" can mean very different things: a large signing bonus inflates year one and vanishes afterwards, while a back-loaded grant does the reverse. Build the actual year-by-year table for each offer — base, plus that year's vesting equity at a stated share price, plus that year's signing instalment, plus target bonus — and compare year 1, year 2 and year 4 side by side. Companies present the number that flatters them, and the difference between two offers frequently reverses once you do this.

Interview angle

The numbers above are illustrative, not benchmarks — bands differ enormously by company, level, location and year, and anything written down goes stale fast. Get your actual ranges from current sources: levels.fyi and similar aggregators for public data, and more usefully, people you know at that company and level. The method on this page is what transfers; the figures are not.

Practice
  • Build the year-by-year table for any offer you receive, before you reply to it. It takes ten minutes and it regularly changes the ranking.
  • Work out what your current package is worth on the same basis. Most people cannot say, which makes comparison impossible.

Negotiating

Negotiation makes people uncomfortable and so they skip it, which is expensive: the first number is rarely the best number, and recruiters expect a conversation. Done politely, it has essentially no downside — offers are not withdrawn because a candidate asked.

  1. Do not give a number first, for as long as you reasonably can. The earlier you anchor, the more likely you anchor low. Redirect to the band for the level — see the deflection on the recruiter screen page.
  2. Be enthusiastic and specific. "I want to work here, and I would like to make the numbers work" is the whole tone. Adversarial framing makes recruiters defensive and is not necessary.
  3. Ask for a specific thing, with a reason. "Could you look at the equity component?" with a reason attached beats "is there any flexibility?", which invites a no.
  4. Competing offers are the strongest lever, which is the practical argument for interviewing at several places in the same window rather than sequentially. Be straightforward about them; do not invent one.
  5. Negotiate level before money where you can. A level is worth more over a career than any one-off adjustment, and it compounds into every subsequent band and every future offer.
  6. Get it in writing before you resign anything. Verbal agreements get forgotten, and the person who made it may not be the person who processes it.
The timing that makes this easy

The reason to cluster your loops into a single few-week window is almost entirely this step. Offers typically come with a response deadline, and a competing offer is only leverage while both are live. Starting your least-favourite processes first and your favourite last — which is also what the cooldown argument suggests — tends to land them in roughly the right order. If an offer arrives early and you are still interviewing, it is completely normal to ask for an extension; recruiters grant them routinely, and asking costs nothing.

Do not resign before everything is signed

Written, countersigned, background check cleared, start date agreed. Offers are occasionally rescinded — reorganisations, hiring freezes, failed checks — and a resignation you cannot take back is a bad position to be in. Also read what you are signing: notice periods, non-competes where enforceable, clawbacks on the signing bonus if you leave inside a year or two, and what happens to unvested equity. The signing-bonus clawback in particular surprises people.

Interview angle

If the company genuinely will not move on money, there are other things that cost them less and may be worth more to you: a start date that gives you a month off, a sign-on adjustment rather than a base change, a written early review at six months, team placement, or remote or relocation terms. Asking "if the band is fixed, what else is flexible?" is a good question and often produces something. And if the answer is genuinely nothing — some companies run strict levelling bands by policy — then you have learned that, and you can decide on the offer as it stands rather than wondering.

Practice
  • Write your counter-offer email before you need it. Three sentences: enthusiasm, the specific ask, the reason.
  • Rehearse the "what are you looking for?" deflection until it is comfortable. It is the moment that sets the anchor.

If it does not land

Most people fail several loops before they pass one, including people who are clearly good at the job. The process has a high false-negative rate by design — companies optimise against hiring the wrong person, not against missing the right one — so a rejection carries much less information about you than it feels like it does.

What is worth doing afterwards:

  • Ask for feedback. Often you get nothing useful for legal reasons, occasionally you get a sentence that is worth the asking. It costs one email.
  • Write your own debrief within 24 hours, while you remember. Every question, how it went, where you stalled, what you would do differently. This is far more valuable than official feedback and nobody can withhold it.
  • Find the pattern across loops. One rejection is noise. Three rejections that all stalled in the system design round is a signal, and it tells you exactly what to work on.
  • Note the cooldown and diarise the date. Reapplying is normal and people do get hired on a second or third attempt.
The variance is real

Outcomes depend on which interviewers you drew, which problems they chose, whether the team was hiring urgently, how you slept, and how the first round went. The same candidate can get a strong hire at one company and a rejection at a comparable one in the same month. This is not a reason to prepare less — preparation is what moves your average — but it is a reason not to over-read any single result, and a strong reason to run several processes rather than pinning everything on one.

Interview angle

The most useful habit after any loop, passed or failed, is the self-debrief. Write down every question you were asked, what you said, and the moment it started going well or badly. Across three or four loops this becomes the most accurate map of your weaknesses you will ever have — more accurate than mock feedback, because it is drawn from the real thing. It also makes the next loop materially easier, because question types repeat far more than people expect, and you will have already thought about most of what you are asked.

Practice
  • Write the self-debrief within 24 hours of every loop. Keep them in one file.
  • After three loops, read them together and find the pattern. Prepare against that, not against a generic plan.
