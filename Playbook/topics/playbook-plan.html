The Twelve-Week Plan

A schedule across all four tracks, with the ratios shifting by phase and by the level you are targeting — plus the drill tracker that measures the one thing that actually predicts your performance: the fraction of problems you can solve unaided.

Four principles that decide whether the plan works

Most preparation plans fail the same four ways. Fix these and the schedule almost does not matter; ignore them and no schedule helps.

  1. Recall, not recognition. Reading a solution produces the feeling of understanding and almost none of the ability. You have learned a problem when you can reproduce it cold a week later, from the idea alone. Everything below is built around re-solving rather than re-reading.
  2. Spaced repetition over volume. Two hundred problems solved once is worse preparation than eighty problems solved three times at widening intervals. The second and third passes are where the pattern actually lands, and they are the passes people skip because they feel less productive.
  3. Out loud, always. Every round is scored partly on communication, and silent practice trains the opposite habit. If you do nothing else differently, narrate.
  4. Proportional to your level. A senior candidate grinding algorithms and a mid-level candidate reading distributed systems papers are both optimising the thing that cannot move their outcome. See the weighting table.
Twelve weeks is a shape, not a prescription

If you have six weeks, run the plan below at double pace and cut the second pass on the lower-frequency topics. If you have six months, run it once and then repeat the drill cycle. If you are already strong in one track, compress it to a revision pass and spend the time on your weakest. The ordering is what matters: foundations before patterns, patterns before mixed practice, and mock interviews in the last third rather than at the end.

Interview angle

The single most common planning error is leaving mocks until the final week. Interviewing is a separate skill from problem-solving, it is learned only by doing it under observation, and the feedback from your first mock will change what you spend the rest of your preparation on. Do your first mock in week five, when you are not ready — being bad at it is the point, and finding out in week five is recoverable in a way that finding out in week eleven is not.

Practice
  • Be honest about which of the four principles you currently violate. It is usually the first two.

Adjusting for your level

Entry / new grad: push coding to 70–75% throughout and treat system design as familiarity rather than mastery. Senior: flip the weeks 7–12 ratios — system design, the deep-dive and behavioural should take over half your time, and coding becomes maintenance to stay sharp rather than a growth area. Returning after a long gap: add three weeks in front for the DSA foundations page and basic fluency, because everything downstream is slower if you are fighting the language.

Interview angle

Track completion rather than hours. "I studied for twelve hours this week" is not a measurement; "I solved fourteen problems, eleven unaided, and three went into the repeat deck" is. The second number — unaided — is the one that actually predicts your performance, because the interview is unaided by definition. If it is not climbing week over week, more hours of the same activity will not fix it; change the activity.

Practice
  • Put the twelve weeks in a calendar with specific sessions, not a vague intention. Protect the mock slots first — they are the ones that get dropped.
  • Define your own weekly milestone check and actually run it. A plan with no check is a wish.

The drill tracker

The visited-page markers on the four sites tell you what you have read. They do not tell you what you can do, and that gap is where most preparation quietly fails. Keep a separate, deliberately low-tech record of problems, and make the repeat schedule explicit.

Three columns do almost all the work: when you last attempted it, whether you got it unaided, and when it is due again.

# drills.md  — one line per problem. Plain text on purpose: the point is
# that it is cheap enough that you actually keep it up.
#
# Status:  U = solved unaided
#          H = needed a hint or a nudge
#          F = looked at the solution
#
# Repeat schedule (double the gap each time you get a U):
#   F or H  ->  retry in 2 days
#   first U ->  +1 week
#   second U - > +3 weeks
#   third U ->  retired; you know it
#
| Problem                  | Pattern        | Last  | St | Due   | Notes                       |
|--------------------------|----------------|-------|----|-------|-----------------------------|
| Longest substring k dist | Sliding window | 03-02 | U  | 03-09 | shrink condition took 2 goes|
| Course schedule II       | Topo sort      | 03-02 | H  | 03-04 | forgot the cycle check      |
| Coin change II           | Knapsack       | 03-03 | F  | 03-05 | loop nesting — combinations |
| Word search              | Backtracking   | 03-04 | U  | 03-11 | remembered to un-mark       |
| LRU cache                | Design         | 03-04 | U  | 03-25 | 2nd U — sentinels help      |

WEEKLY REVIEW (fifteen minutes, same slot every week):
  1. Count this week's U / H / F. Is the U fraction climbing?
  2. Any pattern with two or more F? That is next week's focus — not
     more problems, but re-reading that topic page and re-deriving it.
  3. Anything due that you skipped? Do it first, before new problems.
  4. One sentence: what was the single biggest gap this week?

Why the "unaided" column is the only one that matters

Every other metric — problems attempted, hours spent, topics covered — can go up while your actual readiness stays flat, because they are all satisfied by reading solutions. The fraction you solve unaided cannot be gamed: it is the same thing the interview measures. If that fraction is not rising across weeks, something about your method is wrong, and the usual culprit is reaching for the solution too early. Set a hard rule — twenty-five minutes of genuine attempt before you look at anything — and the number starts moving.

Signal in the trackerWhat it meansWhat to change
Lots of F in one patternYou have not learned the pattern, only the problemsRe-read the topic page; re-derive the canonical solution from scratch
U on first attempt, F on the repeatYou recognised it rather than recalling itLengthen the gaps; stop solving the same problem too soon
U fraction flat across weeksReading too early, or practising only what you can already doHard 25-minute rule; deliberately pick harder problems
Nothing ever retiresGaps are too short, or you are not getting three UsFollow the schedule; three clean Us really is enough
Tracker abandoned after week 2It was too elaborateGo back to five columns in a text file
Interview angle

Two habits that are worth more than any tooling. First, write the note — the last column. "Forgot the cycle check" is a sentence that stops you forgetting the cycle check, and skimming those notes before a round is the most efficient revision available to you. Second, keep a separate one-page list of your own recurring mistakes, across all patterns: off-by-one in binary search, forgetting to un-mark in backtracking, not handling the empty input. That page is usually shorter than people expect — most of us have about six characteristic errors — and reading it the morning of an interview is worth more than any last-minute problem.

Practice
  • Start the tracker today, even with three rows. The habit is what you are building, not the file.
  • Write your personal recurring-mistakes page. Add to it every time a mock surfaces one.
  • Set the hard 25-minute rule and keep it for one week. Watch what happens to the unaided fraction.
