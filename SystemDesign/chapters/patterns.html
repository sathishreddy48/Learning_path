The 28 chapters in these notes describe 28 systems. They do not describe 28 ideas — the same dozen keep reappearing in different costumes, and a question that looks unfamiliar is usually a familiar pattern with new nouns.

This page is an index of those patterns, assembled from the chapters rather than added on top of them. Each entry states the pattern, a question that tends to identify it, and the chapters where it appears — so that recognising a pattern points you at the chapters that already work through it.

Nothing here is new material; it is cross-references collected in one place.

1. Fan-out: pay on write, or pay on read

One event must reach many places. You can precompute every recipient's view when the event happens (push / fan-out on write), or assemble it when each recipient asks (pull / fan-out on read).

Push (fan-out on write) Pull (fan-out on read)
Work per event O(recipients) O(1)
Work per read O(1) O(sources)
Cost driven by Users with many followers Users who follow many sources
Wasted work Writing for people who never look Recomputing the same view repeatedly
Breaks when One source has millions of recipients Everyone has many sources and reads often

The question to ask: how skewed is the fan-out distribution? If it is uniform, pick whichever side is cheaper. If it has a long tail — one account with ten million followers — no single choice works and you need a hybrid, or a cap.

Where it appears

Chapter The fan-out Resolution
11 – News Feed Post → followers' feeds Hybrid — push for ordinary users, pull for celebrities
12 – Chat Message → group members' inboxes Push, made safe by the 100-member cap
10 – Notifications Notification → a user's devices Push; the cap is devices per user
17 – Nearby Friends Location update → friends Push, with a 5,000-friend cap; most of it discarded by a distance filter
23 – Email One email → recipients' mailboxes Push, with no alternative — federation means you cannot ask another provider to compute a view

One thing the table shows: wherever these chapters choose push, a cap on fan-out appears alongside it — chapter 12's 100-member groups, chapter 17's 5,000 friends, chapter 10's devices per user. Chapter 11, which has no cap, is the one that needs a hybrid. Both chapters say so directly, and it is worth checking a requirement for an implied cap before deciding which side to pay on.

2. An immutable log plus a derived view

Store what happened as an append-only sequence, and treat current state as a projection of it that can be thrown away and rebuilt.

What the chapters cite in its favour: appending is the cheapest access pattern a disk has (Chapter 19 quantifies it at two to three orders of magnitude over random I/O), history is auditable because nothing is overwritten, and the view stops being precious because it can be rebuilt.

The question to ask: which of these two things cannot be regenerated? That one must be durable and replicated. Everything else is an optimisation.

Chapter The log The derived view
19 – Message Queue The partition log itself Consumer offsets
6 – Key-Value Store Commit log, then SSTables Memtable, Bloom filters, compacted files
21 – Ad Clicks Raw click events Per-minute aggregates
25 – Leaderboard Durable score log Redis sorted set
26 – Payments Double-entry ledger Wallet balance
27 – Digital Wallet Event list Account balances, snapshots
28 – Stock Exchange Sequenced event stream Order book
24 – Object Storage Immutable packed files Object mapping index

Three consequences that come with the pattern every time:

  • Replay cost grows forever, so you need periodic snapshots — and a snapshot must never become a source of truth.
  • Replay must be deterministic, or the view you rebuild is not the view you had. Every non-deterministic input (a clock, a random value, an external call) has to be resolved before the entry is written and recorded inside it. Chapter 27 and Chapter 28 both rest on this.
  • Deletion stops being deletion. With nothing mutable, an absence is recorded by appending a marker — a tombstone in Chapter 6, a delete marker in Chapter 24, a reversing entry in Chapter 26 — and space comes back later, via compaction or garbage collection.

3. At-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing

Across these chapters, duplicate delivery is treated as something to expect rather than prevent: queues redeliver, clients retry, and an ambiguous failure is indistinguishable from a lost message. What they do instead is deliver at least once and make the processing idempotent, which yields exactly-once effects. Chapter 19 sets out what true exactly-once delivery would cost and where it stops applying.

The question to ask: what enforces the uniqueness — the storage engine, or application code? A check-then-act in application code races; a unique constraint cannot.

Chapter The idempotency key Enforced by
26 – Payments Client-supplied idempotency-key Unique index, plus the stored response
22 – Hotel Reservation reservation_id, created before submission Unique constraint
12 – Chat client_msg_id Server-side dedup
21 – Ad Clicks (ad_id, minute) Upsert instead of increment — replay changes nothing
19 – Message Queue Producer ID plus sequence number Broker-side dedup
10 – Notifications Event ID, within a TTL window Atomic conditional write
23 – Email Message-Id Receiver-side dedup

Four details that the chapters raise as things to get right, set out most fully in Chapter 26:

  1. The key must be generated by the client, before the first attempt — a server-generated key differs per retry and deduplicates nothing.
  2. Store the response, not just the key, or a retry cannot return the same answer.
  3. A reused key with a different payload must error, not silently return the old result, or a genuinely different request vanishes.
  4. Keys expire, and a retry after the window creates a duplicate. Pick a window longer than any realistic retry path.

Note the wording these chapters settle on. Chapter 19 is explicit that exactly-once delivery requires a transaction coordinator and still stops at the system boundary, and chapters 10, 21 and 26 all arrive at at-least-once delivery with idempotent processing instead. "At-least-once plus idempotent processing" is the formulation they use, and it describes what the designs actually do.

4. A hot key is not a partitioning problem

Consistent hashing, sharding and partitioning all distribute keys. None of them distribute requests to a single key. One viral post, one dense map cell, one popular merchant, one in-demand hotel-date — each concentrates on whichever node owns that key, and adding nodes does nothing.

The question to ask: does an invariant live in this key? That decides which remedies are available.

Remedy Works when Cost
Replicate the hot entry It is read-heavy and immutable-ish More copies to keep consistent
Cache it locally on every server Reads dominate Staleness, memory on every node
Salt / bucket the key, aggregate in two stages The operation is associative (counting, summing) Reads become scatter-gather
Deliberately serialise it with a queue Writes must be ordered anyway A hard throughput ceiling, accepted knowingly
Chapter The hot key Why it cannot be split
5 – Consistent Hashing — States the general result: it balances keys, not traffic
11 – News Feed A viral post in the content cache — (replicate or cache locally)
16 – Proximity A dense geohash cell —
21 – Ad Clicks A viral ad Counting is associative, so salting works
22 – Hotel One (hotel, room type, date) row The capacity invariant is in that row
25 – Leaderboard The whole leaderboard is one Redis key Rank requires a single ordering
27 – Digital Wallet A popular merchant's account The balance invariant is in that account
28 – Stock Exchange The sequencer A total order cannot be sharded

What separates the rows is whether an invariant lives in the key. Counting a viral ad is associative, so chapter 21 can salt it and sum the parts; a hotel's remaining capacity and a wallet's balance are the values being protected, so chapters 22 and 27 cannot split them and accept a ceiling instead.

5. Does the shard key contain the transaction?

Sharding is easy when every operation touches exactly one shard, and painful the moment one operation crosses a boundary — because that is precisely what a shard boundary is designed to prevent.

The question to ask, before proposing any shard key: name the operations, and check whether each stays inside one shard.

Chapter Shard key Contained? Consequence
22 – Hotel hotel_id Yes — a booking never spans hotels Keeps ACID transactions while scaling out
23 – Email user_id Yes — every mail operation is one mailbox Simple; but no shared mailboxes
15 – Google Drive user_id No — a shared file spans two users Sharing is what makes the metadata store hard
24 – Object Storage hash(bucket, object) Yes for lookup, no for listing A second, differently-sharded listing table
9 – Web Crawler Host, not URL Politeness state is per host Sharding by URL would break the rate limit

Chapter 9 is the case where the natural key is the wrong one: partitioning the frontier by URL would leave every node inside its own per-host rate limit while the host was collectively overwhelmed. The chapter's conclusion is that the shard key has to contain whatever is being enforced, not only the data.

6. Cheap over-approximation, then an exact check

Narrow a huge candidate set with something fast and imprecise, then pay for precision only on the survivors. The art is being explicit about which direction the error goes — and making sure the harmless direction is the one that happens.

Chapter Cheap filter Error direction Exact check
6 – Key-Value Store Bloom filter False positive = a wasted disk read Read the SSTable
8 – URL Shortener Bloom filter on short keys False positive = pick another key Unique index
9 – Web Crawler Bloom filter on seen URLs False positive = a page never crawled — (accepted, and measured)
16 – Proximity 9 geohash cells (a square) Over-inclusive Haversine distance filter
18 – Google Maps Coarse super-tile containment False positive = one route checked needlessly Exact route membership
9 – Web Crawler SimHash fingerprint distance Near-duplicate threshold —

The error direction is what varies, and chapter 9's row is where it costs most: a false positive there means a page is never crawled, with nothing logged. The chapters that use these filters all state the direction explicitly and size the filter against an accepted rate.

7. A cache is only as good as how many requests share a key

Cache effectiveness is governed by key cardinality, not cache size. A key nobody else will ever request is not a cache entry; it is a memory leak with a TTL.

The question to ask: how many requests can share this key? If the answer is "about one", reduce the key's precision until it is more.

Chapter Bad key Good key Why
16 – Proximity Raw GPS coordinates Geohash cell Everyone on a block shares one entry; GPS jitter stops mattering
13 – Autocomplete prefix + user prefix Personalization makes the answer unshareable and unprecomputable
14 – YouTube — Tile / segment URL Immutable, so cacheable at every layer
20 – Metrics Labels including user_id Bounded label values Cardinality is a product; one unbounded label kills the system

Chapter 20 is the same lesson pointed at storage rather than caching: series count is the product of label cardinalities, memory scales with series count, and one high-cardinality label multiplies it by millions.

8. Time-ordered IDs cut both ways

Prefixing an identifier with a timestamp buys index locality, sortability and cursor pagination. It also makes every new write land next to every other new write.

Benefit Where
Index locality — inserts append instead of scattering 7 – Unique ID
Cursor pagination over a growing list 11 – News Feed
Merging two sources in time order 11 – News Feed (pushed + pulled posts)
Sync cursors — "everything after this" 12 – Chat, 15 – Drive
Cost Where
Range-sharding sends every write to the newest shard 7 – Unique ID
Sequential key prefixes make a hot partition 24 – Object Storage
k-sortable is not totally ordered — ties within a millisecond are arbitrary 12 – Chat
The ID leaks creation time, volume, and enumerable neighbours 8 – URL Shortener

Chapters 11 and 12 draw the line between the two columns. Snowflake IDs are sufficient to paginate a feed and, chapter 12 argues, insufficient to order a conversation — within one millisecond the order is arbitrary — so it uses a strictly-ordered per-channel counter. "Sortable" and "totally ordered" are different guarantees.

And the related choice for collecting data, which has the opposite answer from what you might expect:

Chapter Direction Why
19 – Message Queue Consumers pull Consumers set their own rate and cannot be overwhelmed

Chapter 20's reasoning turns on who holds the inventory: with pull, the collector knows what should answer, so silence identifies a dead target; with push, silence could be a dead service, a dead agent, a broken network or an intentional decommission.

10. Stateful tiers are the exception, and they cost

Almost everything in these notes is stateless and therefore boring to scale. The exceptions are memorable because they are where the operational pain lives.

Chapter What holds state What it costs
12 – Chat Live WebSockets per device Losing a server drops ~100k clients, who all reconnect at once
17 – Nearby Friends Sockets plus subscriptions Reconnection is expensive — re-fetch friends, re-subscribe to hundreds of channels
16 – Proximity Quadtree built in-process Minutes of startup before a server can serve; staggered rollouts
25 – Leaderboard Redis as the system of record Persistence and replication become correctness concerns
27 – Digital Wallet Local event log Node identity matters; restores take as long as replay
28 – Stock Exchange Everything, on one machine One failure domain; recovery is replay

The shared consequences: deploys become incidents (every restart is a mass reconnection), clients need jittered backoff or recovery cascades, and servers need draining rather than termination. If a design has a stateful tier, those three belong in the answer.

11. Sometimes the business absorbs the inconsistency

Several chapters turn out to have softer constraints than they first appear, and the distinguishing question is what the business does today when the constraint is violated. Where there is already a procedure, the requirement is negotiable and much cheaper to meet; where there is not, it is absolute.

Chapter The constraint Absorbable?
22 – Hotel Never oversell rooms Yes — overbook 10%, and walk the guest if it happens
11 – News Feed Everyone sees the same feed Yes — eventual consistency is invisible
20 – Metrics Exact counts Yes — approximate is fine, and cheaper
17 – Nearby Friends Deliver every location update Yes, explicitly — and that one sentence licenses the entire design
27 – Digital Wallet Money is never created or destroyed No
26 – Payments Never double-charge No

Chapter 17 is the clearest case: "occasional data point loss is acceptable" is what permits fire-and-forget pub/sub, no acknowledgements, no retries and TTL-based presence. Remove that sentence and the design has to become Chapter 19.

12. Detect-and-correct, when you cannot prevent

When an operation spans systems that share no transaction — your database, a third-party API, a bank — you cannot make them consistent. You can guarantee that divergence is found and fixed within a bounded time, and that is a real guarantee rather than a consolation.

Chapter What cannot be made atomic The detection mechanism
26 – Payments Your DB, the PSP, the bank Nightly settlement-file reconciliation; ledger-vs-wallet checks
21 – Ad Clicks Streaming aggregation vs reality End-of-day batch recomputation — the authoritative number
27 – Digital Wallet Shards in a distributed transfer Replay from the event log; reconciliation at external boundaries
6 – Key-Value Store Replicas during a partition Merkle-tree anti-entropy and read repair

In these chapters reconciliation is load-bearing rather than remedial: chapter 26 notes that no transaction spans its database, the PSP and a bank, and chapter 21 treats its nightly batch recomputation as the authoritative figure that the streaming result is checked against. Both also treat an unclassifiable mismatch as a signal that a failure mode has not been modelled yet.

13. Scale out is an answer to one kind of constraint

Twenty-seven chapters scale out, because their constraint is volume. Chapter 28 scales in — one machine, one pinned thread, shared memory, no disk, no locks — because its constraint is the latency of a single decision, and one network round trip costs more than its entire budget.

Read against Chapter 1, the contrast shows the constraint deciding the shape: chapter 1 adds servers because requests exceed one machine's capacity, and chapter 28 removes them because a single network hop exceeds its whole latency budget. Both are responses to a measured number, and chapter 28 is the reminder that distribution answers one kind of number rather than being the default.

Using this page

In an interview, after gathering requirements and doing the estimation:

  1. Name the pattern. "This is a fan-out problem." "This is an immutable-log-plus-projection problem." That sentence organises everything after it.
  2. Ask the pattern's question. How skewed is the distribution? Which of these cannot be regenerated? Does the shard key contain the transaction? What does the business do when this is violated?
  3. Expect the pattern's failure modes, because they recur too: hot keys, duplicate delivery, replay cost, cache keys that nobody shares, stateful tiers and reconnection storms.

This is also the convention the chapters themselves are written in: each design step is paired with what it costs as well as what it buys. Carrying that into an answer — naming the price of each choice alongside its benefit — is what the "what broke / what it now costs you" framing throughout these notes is for.
